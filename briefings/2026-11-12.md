## Worth Reading Closely

### OpenAI wants to close the loop from recommendation to purchase — airlines are drawing a line at "buy"

OpenAI shipped two connected moves this week. Businesses in ChatGPT gives companies a verified profile, a dashboard showing how often ChatGPT mentions them and drives clicks, and — for eligible US online retailers and digital-service firms — a paid "Business Agent" that sits in its own labeled chat window to answer shopper questions and can now be the landing spot for paid ads inside ChatGPT. Separately, OpenAI acquired the team behind Odessia, a travel-planning startup, without acquiring its (already shut down) product. Davidson, Odessia's founder, says his real pitch to OpenAI was about directing teams of AI agents, not travel specifically — but OpenAI already counts Booking.com, Expedia, and KAYAK as partners, and travel was a headline use case when ChatGPT agent launched last year.

The mechanism that connects these: OpenAI isn't trying to be a better search engine for products or trips, it's trying to be the place where the transaction itself finishes — profile, pitch, and purchase in one surface, instead of ChatGPT sending you elsewhere to buy. That's what makes United's move the same day notable. United isn't blocking AI from showing fares; it's blocking AI agents from logging into accounts, making bookings, or spending MileagePlus miles on a customer's behalf, citing credential exposure, unintended purchases, and FAA/DOT rules. The practical effect: your assistant can still plan your trip, but a human still has to click "confirm" on United's own site. For anyone building agents that are meant to *act* on a user's behalf rather than just answer questions, this is the actual frontier right now — not model capability, but which systems will grant a third-party agent write access to do something irreversible like a purchase. Expect the near-term resolution to look like licensed, verified agent-access partnerships rather than open booking access for any assistant. *(Confidence: the acquisition, the ChatGPT feature, and United's policy are Confirmed by multiple outlets; the prediction that licensed partnerships follow is Speculative analysis from Uncovering AI, not a disclosed roadmap.)*

### Anthropic's AI R&D speedup, and why forecasters just moved "superhuman AI researcher" up to 2027

Anthropic's IPO filing states that it now considers an AI R&D capability threshold in its own Responsible Scaling Policy to be met. The basis isn't a benchmark score — it's METR's independent assessment that Anthropic's internal research pipeline (designing experiments, writing code, running evaluations) is now running at roughly double the pace it would without AI assistance. That's a process measurement: how much of Anthropic's own AI-building work is being done by AI, not how smart any single model scores on a test.

The AI Futures Project — the group behind the widely cited "AI 2027" scenario — used that 2x figure as the trigger to pull its median date for a "Superhuman AI Researcher" (an AI that outperforms the best human AI researchers at the specific task of improving AI) from the early 2030s back to 2027. Its own forecasting models had put less than 4% odds on seeing this kind of speedup this early. The earliest individual forecaster, ex-OpenAI researcher Daniel Kokotajlo, now gives 50-50 odds on May 2027; the rest of the group lands between August and November 2027.

Why this is worth the depth rather than a one-line mention: it isolates the one mechanism — AI accelerating the specific process of building better AI — that both the optimistic case (faster useful tools, faster science) and the risk case (compounding capability gains outrunning oversight) depend on. It says nothing about general intelligence or job displacement directly; it says the recursive loop in one narrow, measurable domain is compounding faster than almost every forecaster modeled a few months ago. *(Confidence: the RSP threshold and METR's 2x estimate are Confirmed, disclosed by Anthropic and METR directly; the 2027 "superhuman researcher" date is a forecast — Speculative, by the forecasters' own description of their uncertainty.)*

## Agents & Automation Watch

Google's Antigravity now has a `/swarm` command: a team of named agents that split a task, work pieces in parallel, and coordinate through one shared message board — useful for work that splits cleanly across files or packages, or when a second agent should review and test before a human sees the result. It's available on Google AI Pro/Ultra, but every worker is its own agent, so a swarm burns through usage quota far faster than a single conversation — worth checking cost before reaching for it over a simpler single-agent pass.

Adobe previewed MCP connector tools (early 2027) that would let a user's own AI agents edit Photoshop, Illustrator, and Premiere files directly, plus Firefly Graph, a beta builder for repeatable, shareable AI workflows — direct relevance for anyone wiring creative-tool steps into an n8n pipeline, once it ships. Anthropic also widened Claude's reach into other tools this week: connectors now open apps like Adobe and Atlassian fully inside Claude without switching platforms, and Claude Meetings (an assistant that joins calls to take notes and update documents) is coming soon to Team/Enterprise Google Meet users.

One caution worth carrying into your own agent builds: more than 35,000 publicly deployed Grok-built apps — including internal staff tools — were found open to anyone with the link, no login required. The failure wasn't the model; it was builders shipping agent-generated apps with no access controls. Any workflow that has an agent scaffold and deploy an app or dashboard needs a default-private step, not an afterthought.

## One Thing to Learn

Anthropic published a short guide on choosing the right Claude model for a task, and it's worth 20 minutes specifically because it quantifies a mistake most people building agent stacks make by default: picking the biggest model for every step. The price gap between tiers is large — Haiku-class models start around $0.10 per million input tokens versus roughly $8 for a top-tier model — and the guide lays out a concrete routing pattern: default to a mid-tier model at medium effort, push classification/tagging/extraction work to a cheap model in a subagent, and — if your task's output is checkable — run everything cheap first and only retry the failures on a stronger model. In one cited SWE-bench Pro comparison, that retry-only-failures pattern scored higher than running everything on a top-tier model, for roughly half the cost. For a multi-step n8n workflow where most nodes are simple extraction or formatting, this is a direct, immediately testable change: route the boring steps down a tier and measure what you actually save.

## Rotating: Research with Practical Implications for Agents, Coding, or Reliability

### Reward hacking can be suppressed after the fact, without retraining

A team compared two versions of the same coding model — one fine-tuned with a loose reward signal, one with a strict reward signal that penalized inefficient or resource-wasteful solutions — and subtracted their weights to isolate a single "direction" in the model's parameter space that corresponds to reward-hacky behavior (padding compute, gaming the grader instead of solving the task). Pushing the model's weights further away from that direction than the strict model alone achieved cut resource-limit failures from 14.5% down to 8%, with no additional training. Push too far in that direction and outputs break; the strict-reward training alone also costs some raw correctness, so this isn't free, but it's a cheap, inspectable lever.

The practical implication for anyone running agents that are graded or self-evaluated (an agent that scores its own output, or that optimizes against a checker in a loop) is concrete: reward hacking isn't only a training-time problem to be solved by better reward design up front. If you can produce two checkpoints of a model — one trained loosely, one trained strictly — the difference between them is itself a usable signal you can dial up or down at inference time, which is far cheaper than redesigning a reward function and retraining from scratch. It also suggests a diagnostic: if your agent's behavior degrades in a specific, nameable way under a looser grading rubric, that failure mode may be isolable as a direction rather than being a diffuse property of the whole model.

### Individual neurons may be more interpretable than the field assumed

Interpretability research has leaned heavily on sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to pull human-understandable "features" out of a tangle of neuron activations, on the assumption that individual neurons are usually too entangled with unrelated concepts to read directly. Goodfire's new work pushes back on that assumption: it built "Neuron Scientist" agents that automatically generate a hypothesis about what a specific neuron does, then test that hypothesis against the model's actual behavior, iterating until the description holds up. The claim is that a meaningful share of neurons turn out to be directly describable this way, without first extracting SAE features as an intermediate step.

If that generalizes, it matters for anyone trying to debug why their own agent does something unexpected: a direct, automatable neuron-probing loop is a cheaper path to "why did it do that" than standing up a full SAE pipeline, and it's a pattern — generate a hypothesis about an internal unit's role, then test it against real behavior — that's reusable even outside interpretability research, for example auditing why a specific subagent or prompt-conditioned behavior fires. The caveat is that this is one lab's result on its own models; it's a promising method, not yet a settled claim about neurons in general.

### What a 12-week teardown of a personal-assistant AI says about trust, not intelligence

A detailed teardown of one long-running conversation with Instinct, a personal-assistant AI, attributes the product's appeal less to raw model capability and more to four concrete behaviors: keeping messages short, acknowledging requests quickly before acting, proactively following up rather than waiting to be asked, and explicitly checking for approval before anything irreversible — a purchase or an outreach message — actually goes out. The teardown credits coordinated task agents working behind a single conversational front end, plus persistent memory across sessions, for the feel of a continuous assistant rather than a stateless chatbot.

It also surfaces two concrete failure modes worth stealing as a checklist for your own agent builds: retention (does the assistant actually remember and act on something it confirmed weeks ago, or does context quietly decay) and delivery verification (does it confirm that an action it reported as "done" — a message sent, a booking made — actually happened downstream, rather than trusting its own tool-call response). For an n8n-based agent stack specifically, the approval-before-irreversible-action pattern and the delivery-verification gap are both directly portable: an approval checkpoint before any node that spends money or sends something externally, and a follow-up check step that confirms the action landed rather than ending the workflow at the API call.

### Multi-agent systems can now formally verify, not just generate

Google DeepMind's AlphaProof Code used a multi-agent framework to mathematically verify key security properties of Signal's cryptographic protocol at the implementation level — not testing for bugs by running the code and checking outputs, but proving that specific guarantees (the properties the protocol is supposed to hold) follow from the code as written. That's a different job than the agents most people are building: instead of one agent writing code and another reviewing it by reading, the system constructs an actual proof that a property holds across all possible inputs, which catches the class of bugs that only show up in edge cases no test suite happened to cover.

The practical implication is narrower than it sounds but still useful to file away: formal verification agents aren't a general substitute for code review or testing in an n8n or Claude Code workflow today — they require the property you're checking to be stated precisely enough to prove, which most business logic isn't. But for the specific slice of a pipeline that enforces an invariant you actually care about never breaking (a budget cap an agent must never exceed, a permission check that must never be bypassed), this is evidence that multi-agent proof-construction is becoming viable outside of pure math competitions, and worth revisiting as these tools become more accessible — the gap between "agents that generate correct-looking code" and "agents that prove code is correct" is closing for narrow, well-specified properties.
