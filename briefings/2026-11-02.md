## Worth Reading Closely

### Claude's drug-development push, and where it actually broke

Anthropic's disclosure this week wasn't just "Claude helps with biotech." It bought Mithril Medicine, a one-person company founded by Cody Tranbarger, who used Claude to run the coordination layer of drug development — organizing contracts, scheduling, and experiment logistics across 11 outside labs in under 18 months, instead of the multi-year, full-team timeline that process usually takes. The treatment targets LAMA2-related congenital muscular dystrophy, a rare muscle disease in children, and the mouse-study results are a small pilot, still preclinical.

The mechanism worth understanding is *where* Claude added value and where it didn't. At Enveda, Claude caught 7 issues in manufacturing paperwork that human chemists — who were spending up to 75% of their time on forms — had missed. In lab-dish tests, Claude-assisted work pushed immune cells to produce roughly 10x as many cancer-spotting receptors. That's the upside case: an AI handling the coordination and documentation overhead that eats specialist time, freeing PhDs to do PhD-level work.

But Anthropic's own report flagged six errors Claude made during the process, and at least three of those errors reached outside labs — in Claude-drafted emails that the team had reviewed, edited, and sent anyway. That detail matters more than the headline. The failure wasn't Claude hallucinating in isolation; it was a human review step that didn't catch what it was supposed to catch before the email went out. For anyone building agent workflows (this is the direct lesson for an n8n/Claude/GitHub stack): the risk isn't just model error, it's "approval theater" — a human-in-the-loop step that exists on paper but isn't actually scrutinizing output. The fix isn't removing the human checkpoint; it's making sure that checkpoint is doing real work. *(Confirmed — Anthropic research disclosure, via Vaibhav Sisinty)*

### The Theranos comparison for AI funding, and why it's harder to pull off twice

Tyler Shultz's TechCrunch interview is an opinion piece, not a data release, but the mechanism-level argument holds up: Theranos worked because (1) due diligence couldn't distinguish real tech from a thin wrapper, (2) profit wasn't a prerequisite for a sky-high valuation, and (3) "trade secrets" was an acceptable reason to withhold proof. Builder.ai, a Microsoft-backed startup valued around $1.5B, collapsed into insolvency in 2025 after its sales were found inflated by roughly 300%, with much of its "AI" work reportedly done by human engineers — a direct modern instance of the same pattern.

Shultz's own argument, though, contains the reason this is harder to repeat at scale: you can test almost any AI product yourself in 30 seconds by typing into it, which Theranos's blood-testing hardware never allowed. That means the "the tech doesn't work at all" fraud is actually harder in AI than it was in biotech. What's still easy to fake is everything *around* the model: one-off pilot revenue dressed up as annual recurring contracts, "proprietary AI" that's an API call to OpenAI or Anthropic with a UI on top, and agent demos that are scripted or quietly human-assisted.

For a finance student evaluating AI companies, the practical takeaway is a diligence checklist, not a vibe check: ask what the product does without the underlying model provider, ask for revenue broken out by contract length (not just topline growth), and treat "we can't disclose our architecture, it's a trade secret" as a red flag rather than a moat — real moats in AI today tend to be data, distribution, or workflow integration, not secret model weights. *(Reported — Tyler Shultz interview via TechCrunch, relayed by Uncovering AI; the fraud-risk framing is Shultz's opinion, not a confirmed finding about any specific company)*

## Agents & Automation Watch

Three items today are directly about agents doing real operational work, with a consistent theme: agents are eating coordination work, not technical work. Asana CEO Dan Rogers runs his calendar, inbox, and competitive-intel review through a squad of agents, plus an analytics agent that pulls sales dashboards he used to request from a BI team. His stated lesson — agents need detailed instructions and active governance to be useful, and Asana restricts what its business-analyst agent can access — is the same lesson as the Claude/Mithril Medicine story above: unsupervised agent output is a liability, supervised-and-scoped agent output is leverage. Take Rogers's framing with a grain of salt, though — he's demoing his own company's product to a national outlet.

SETI's Allen Telescope Array is a cleaner, lower-stakes version of the same shift: agents now point and tune 42 radio dishes from natural-language instructions and monitor incoming data (roughly 60 HD movies' worth per second) for errors in real time, catching a Crab Nebula pulsar as it happened. The task was previously bottlenecked by how many trained humans could staff the controls; it no longer is.

Separately, Proton's CTO told engineers in an internal memo that AI wrote 90% of the company's October code — a claim worth treating skeptically (it's self-reported, unverified, and status-quo-disrupting claims like this tend to get rounder numbers than reality produces), but directionally consistent with where coding agents are heading.

One builder-relevant number: OpenAI is giving eligible ChatGPT Pro and Business subscribers up to $500/month in API credit specifically to build apps and agents — a direct subsidy for exactly the kind of n8n-adjacent agent building this briefing exists for, though unused credit expires monthly rather than banking.

Nothing new today on agent orchestration frameworks, evaluation tooling, or multi-agent reliability research specifically.

## One Thing to Learn

**Claude Skills — packaged, reusable instruction sets you build once and call repeatedly.** Uncovering AI's newsletter teased (without much detail) a paid breakdown on "training your AI platform with skills" to cut prompting and retyping time — worth the 20 minutes to actually understand the underlying feature rather than wait for someone else's paid writeup. The core idea: instead of re-explaining context, format, and constraints in every prompt, you package that as a named "skill" Claude can invoke on demand, the same way this briefing pipeline likely calls repeatable steps (search Gmail, extract, merge duplicates, enforce word budgets) rather than re-deriving the process from scratch each run.

The direct payoff for an n8n/Claude/GitHub stack: any recurring prompt pattern you're currently copy-pasting or templating in an n8n node is a candidate to become a Skill — invoked consistently, versioned in a repo, and reusable across workflows instead of living as prompt text scattered across automation nodes. Spend the 20 minutes reading Anthropic's own Skills documentation (not a third-party summary) and try converting one recurring prompt in your current pipeline into a Skill definition. It's the same reuse-over-repetition instinct as writing a function instead of copy-pasting code, applied to prompting.

## Rotating: Startups & Funding Worth Knowing

The biggest funding-relevant disclosure this week came from Anthropic's own S-1 filing ahead of its IPO. Revenue per employee crossed $6.6 million, up from $1.4 million a year earlier — a more than fourfold jump in twelve months, which is an unusually clean efficiency signal for a company at this scale (compare that to most Big Tech incumbents, where revenue-per-employee growth of that magnitude over one year would be extraordinary). Alongside the filing, Anthropic's co-founders disclosed they've pledged 80% of their personal equity to charity, and the company has placed 5% of its shares into a public benefit fund ahead of the listing. A pledge timed right before a liquidity event is worth viewing with mild skepticion about motive — it's good optics ahead of an IPO — but the commitment is now on the record in a filing, which is a harder commitment than a press-release promise, and it's a larger pledge than competitors have made.

That filing lands in the same week as the Theranos-whistleblower warning above, and the two are worth reading together rather than separately. Tyler Shultz's argument wasn't about any specific company; it was that the *structure* of current AI funding — trillion-dollar valuations without profit requirements, capital from crossover funds and wealthy individuals who are less equipped to audit a model than a specialist VC would be, and "trade secret" as an acceptable non-answer to diligence questions — creates the same conditions Theranos exploited. Builder.ai is the concrete precedent: a Microsoft-backed startup valued around $1.5B that collapsed into insolvency in 2025 after sales were found inflated by roughly 300%, with much of its "AI" work reportedly done by human engineers behind the interface. For anyone tracking AI-adjacent funding rounds as a finance student, the Anthropic S-1 is the "here's what real, auditable scale looks like" reference point, and Builder.ai is the "here's what it looks like when the numbers are fake" reference point — worth holding both in mind when a new AI startup's funding round crosses your feed.

Market reaction adds a third data point: Alphabet had its worst single stock day since 2004 this week, per Fortune's reporting, which ties it loosely to concerns spanning Google Search's competitive position and energy-sector costs tied to AI infrastructure buildout. This is reported market commentary rather than a confirmed causal analysis — stock moves get multiple competing explanations in real time — but it's a signal that public markets are starting to price AI infrastructure capex as a risk factor, not just a growth story, for even the largest, most profitable AI labs. That's a meaningfully different market mood than the "every AI capex dollar is automatically good news" framing of the last two years, and probably the more important long-term story buried in today's newsletters than any single product launch.

On the private-markets side, there's a smaller but related signal: Mistral giving away its 1-trillion-parameter Large 4 model's weights for free (under a modified MIT license, roughly tying GLM-5.3 as the top open-weight model for agentic coding) is a competitive-pressure move, not a funding event, but it matters for valuation logic — every time a well-funded lab gives away frontier-adjacent weights for free, it narrows the moat argument for any startup whose pitch depends on proprietary model access rather than data, distribution, or workflow integration. That's the same diligence point Shultz raised, just arriving from the supply side instead of the fraud-risk side: if the model itself is increasingly commoditized, the thing actually worth paying for — and worth funding — is everything built around it.

Two smaller signals round out the picture. Google's own benchmark-topping Gemini 4 Carbon is gated to vetted cyber-defense users under a program called Fairwind and shows a notably higher hallucination rate (11%, versus 4% for the prior Gemini 4 Argon) on Artificial Analysis's test — a reminder that "state of the art on a leaderboard" and "ready for a funded startup to build a product on" are not the same claim, and any pitch deck that leans on a benchmark ranking alone deserves a follow-up question about reliability trade-offs. And on the labor side, US undergraduate computer-science enrollment fell 12.4% this fall per the National Student Clearinghouse, while health majors grew 6.2% — a shift worth watching if you're underwriting a startup's ability to hire engineering talent cheaply in the next few years, since a shrinking CS pipeline cuts against the "AI makes junior engineers redundant anyway" narrative some funding decks lean on.
