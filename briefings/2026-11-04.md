## Worth Reading Closely

### An AI edited another AI's brain — and a same-week rebuttal on what that actually proves

Independent researcher Rikiya Takehi gave GPT-6 Astra direct read/write access to the weight tensors of a small, open 0.8B-parameter Qwen model rather than letting it interact through prompts. Astra located a single, specific failure mode — the model looped on its own output and exhausted its context without reaching an answer on roughly 90% of math problems — and corrected it by altering about 0.016% of the model's weight values directly, with no gradient-based training involved. Accuracy jumped from 7.9% to 48.1%, and instruction-following improved too. The same technique barely moved the needle on Google's Gemma 3, which doesn't share that one dominant, legible bug. In smaller tests, Astra could also infer which game a different tiny model had learned purely by inspecting its weights, and write a working Breakout clone from scratch.

The mechanism-level point for anyone building agents: this treats a model as something a more capable model can read and patch, not just prompt — but only under narrow conditions. It worked because the target model was small enough to inspect in full, open enough to edit directly, and broken in one specific, diagnosable way. None of that generalizes yet to patching a frontier model, your own, or anyone else's. A rebuttal published in the same cycle, "LLMs Are Not Superhuman at Reading and Writing Neural Network Weights," pushes back directly on the self-improvement framing: it argues Astra's edit is advanced, fast pattern-matching equivalent to what a skilled ML researcher could do by hand given enough time, not evidence of a qualitatively new capability. The practical read: watch this space for tools that audit or patch small, open, on-device models without a full retrain — not for a model that edits itself. *(Confidence: Confirmed on the experiment itself; Speculative on the "self-improving AI" framing — Rundown AI, TLDR AI, TAAFT)*

### Anthropic's S-1 ties a safety tripwire to the actual dollar figures behind frontier AI

Anthropic's amended IPO prospectus adds specific language that its models could "imminently" cross the company's own internal definition of recursive self-improvement (RSI) — the point where its AI systems meaningfully help build the next, more capable generation of models. The same filing discloses nine-month 2026 revenue of $33.24B, a 1,200% increase year over year, alongside a nine-month net loss of $104B, Q3-only revenue of roughly $17B with a $567M operating loss, and compute and infrastructure commitments of about $895.4B.

What makes this worth reading closely rather than skimming: RSI isn't a vague milestone here, it's a specific threshold Anthropic itself set as an internal safety tripwire, and flagging it in a public securities filing is a disclosure obligation, not a marketing claim — the company is on record saying it may be close. Separately, the financial detail shows how disconnected revenue growth is from profitability at the frontier right now: a 1,200% revenue jump still produced a loss larger than the revenue itself, because multi-year compute commitments are being locked in well ahead of the revenue that's supposed to repay them. For anyone trying to read an AI-adjacent stock, bond, or credit story this quarter, this is the clearest concrete number yet on how a frontier lab finances a buildout that currently outpaces its income. *(Confidence: Confirmed — TLDR AI, Rundown AI)*

## Agents & Automation Watch

Several items this cycle are directly relevant to an n8n/Claude/GitHub stack. Claude's Message Threads (above) is the most actionable: if any of your agents hold a long conversation open across many turns, sending deltas instead of full history is a free latency win worth testing once it's out of beta. *(TLDR AI)*

DeepSeek open-sourced Super-agent, an MIT-licensed "proactive" coding agent that watches a GitHub repo — and optionally a team's Feishu chat — and picks up coding work either when asked directly or when rules the team wrote in advance allow it to act unsupervised. It's a concrete reference implementation of permission-gated autonomous triggers, the same design problem any n8n workflow runs into when deciding what an agent gets to do without a human in the loop. *(Vaibhav Sisinty)*

GitHub shipped Enclaves, a new security primitive for cross-repository agentic workflows: it lets an agent operate across multiple repos while limiting what it can disclose to other agents it talks to, aimed at the risk of an agent quietly leaking private-repo context into an unrelated task. *(TLDR AI)*

Smaller but worth knowing: Docusign's MCP server went GA, letting agents send, track, search, and analyze agreements under user permissions with human approval required on critical steps — one more MCP server to be aware of if document workflows ever enter your stack. And Multiverse Computing's CompactifAI, which runs GLM 5.3 at a lower cost than other providers on Artificial Analysis, ships a native n8n integration and prices repeated context cheaply — directly relevant because agentic workflows tend to resend the same files on every step, which is exactly where their bills pile up. *(TLDR AI, TAAFT)*

## One Thing to Learn

Docusign's MCP server going GA this week is a good excuse to actually learn what MCP (Model Context Protocol) is, if you haven't sat down with the spec yet. It's Anthropic's open standard for how an AI model talks to external tools and data sources — instead of every app building a custom integration for every model, an MCP server exposes a fixed set of "tools" (read a file, send an email, query a database) in a common format that any MCP-compatible client, including Claude and n8n's own MCP nodes, can call the same way.

The 20-minute version worth doing: read through one existing MCP server's tool list (Docusign's or a smaller one, like a GitHub or filesystem server) and notice it's just a short, typed menu of actions with defined inputs and outputs — not magic, just a contract. Once that clicks, every "connect your agent to X" problem you'll hit while building n8n workflows reduces to the same question: does an MCP server already exist for X, and if not, what's the smallest tool contract you'd need to write one yourself. That's the skill that transfers across every agent stack you'll touch, not just Claude's.

## Rotating: Startups & Funding Worth Knowing

**Marvell's $115B bet on AI's physical bottleneck.** Marvell, which designs custom AI chips and the silicon that moves data around a data center, agreed to buy Lumentum, which makes the lasers and optical components that move data between chips and servers, in an all-stock deal valued around $115B — one of the largest tech takeovers on record. The strategic logic is about a bottleneck that's less talked about than GPU supply: as AI clusters scale, the limiting factor increasingly becomes how fast data can physically move between chips, racks, and buildings, not just how many chips exist. Buying rather than partnering with an optics supplier is a bet that connectivity becomes as strategically important — and as worth owning outright — as compute itself. For anyone tracking AI infrastructure as an investment theme, this is a signal that the next wave of consolidation may run through the "boring" physical layer underneath the GPUs, not the chips that get the headlines.

**OpenAI's funding round shrank, and the financing is getting circular.** OpenAI reportedly cut its latest funding round target from $30B to roughly $20B, with about $11B already committed. In the same window, OpenAI signed four agreements in Abu Dhabi — deploying its models inside state oil company ADNOC, the emirate's government (an effort it's calling "Machine-Readable Government"), and state investor Mubadala — while a UAE-linked group is reportedly set to be the largest single investor in that same reduced raise. ADNOC's piece alone aims to cut oil-field planning from months to hours by running decades of well data through OpenAI's models. The pattern worth noticing: Abu Dhabi is simultaneously becoming one of OpenAI's largest customers and, potentially, its largest investor in the same deal cycle — meaning some of the capital funding OpenAI's growth may be coming from, and flowing back to, the same counterparty. That's not unusual in strategic AI partnerships, but it does mean the headline "OpenAI raises $20B" and "OpenAI signs major government AI deal" may be describing overlapping money, not two independent wins. It also means the round's valuation and terms are harder to read as a pure market signal: a sovereign investor who is simultaneously buying tens of billions of dollars of deployment has a reason to price the round generously regardless of what an independent investor would pay, which is worth remembering the next time a headline cites OpenAI's valuation as if it were set by arm's-length competitive bidding.

**S&P just put a number on how long Meta can keep spending like this.** S&P Global cut its outlook on Meta to negative, pointing to roughly $71B of projected negative free cash flow in 2027 as data-center spending keeps climbing faster than the AI business it's funding brings in. "Negative outlook" from a ratings agency isn't a downgrade yet — it's a warning that one becomes more likely if nothing changes, which matters for Meta's borrowing costs on the debt it's already using to fund that buildout. Combined with reports that Meta is weighing raising at least $20B in new bonds specifically for AI infrastructure, this is a concrete, rated-agency estimate of how much runway big tech's AI capex has before it starts showing up as a credit problem rather than just an aggressive growth story.

**The DRAM squeeze is turning into a visible consumer and funding story at once.** Samsung and SK hynix — who together make about 70% of the world's DRAM — signed letters of intent with OpenAI last October for up to 900,000 DRAM wafers a month for its Stargate data centers, which Tom's Hardware estimates at roughly 40% of global DRAM output and analysts have valued above $70B. A letter of intent isn't a binding purchase order, but contract DRAM prices rose sharply afterward anyway. The visible effects: a 32GB DDR5 kit that cost around ₹60,000 in India in August, laptop makers reintroducing 8GB base configurations to hit price points, and a $109 crowdfunded gadget (RAMDeck) pitched at pooling spare RAM from old devices to run larger local AI models — a sign consumers are already improvising around the shortage. Altman's Korea trip this week, chasing both chip supply and the capital to build the data centers around it, is the demand side of the same story that's showing up as a laptop-spec downgrade on the consumer side. For a finance student, this is a clean, current example of how a capital-intensive technology buildout propagates through a commodity supply chain into retail prices within months, not years: the causal chain runs from a letter of intent (not even a binding order) to a contract-price spike, to a laptop spec downgrade, to a crowdfunded workaround product, in under thirteen months.

**A small open-weight model beating a frontier lab on its own benchmark is a funding signal, not just a research result.** Startup Deep Principle released Hydrogen-1.0, an open model built on Z.ai's GLM-5.3, claiming it outperforms GPT-6 Astra on OpenAI's own scientific-research benchmark. Whether or not that claim holds up under independent testing, the funding-relevant point is that a smaller, well-capitalized challenger can now credibly contest a frontier lab's benchmark using someone else's open base model rather than training from scratch — which lowers the capital bar for a startup to make a legitimate competitive claim in a specific domain (here, scientific research) rather than trying to compete on general capability. Worth tracking as a template: pick a narrow benchmark a frontier lab cares about, fine-tune an open model specifically for it, and use that claim to raise on. *(Confirmed — Rundown AI, Vaibhav Sisinty)*
