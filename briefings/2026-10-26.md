## Worth Reading Closely

### SAP's enterprise AI playbook: why agents go "fluently wrong," and how to pay less for them

SAP CTO Philipp Herzig, interviewed by The Rundown AI, laid out a specific mechanism for why AI pilots often never reach production: an agent can give an answer that is fluent, confident, and completely wrong about how the business actually works, because raw table data doesn't tell it what a field means or how entities connect across systems — a supplier record in procurement and a vendor record in finance are the same thing to a person, but not obviously the same thing to a model reading two separate tables. SAP's response is a Knowledge Graph that exposes those relationships and business rules directly to the agent, which Herzig says reduces guessed API calls and lets smaller, cheaper models handle tasks that would otherwise require a frontier model just to infer context no one gave it. A second piece, "Company Memory," stores not just what decision was made on a past exception but why — the rationale and the authority behind it — so the next agent run has precedent to reference instead of starting cold. Herzig is explicit that a human still has to judge whether that exception should become a rule, rather than letting it quietly generalize on its own.

On cost, Herzig's framing cuts against a common instinct: the two biggest sources of AI overspend he's seen are sending routine tasks (an email summary) to a frontier model built for multi-step reasoning, and "context bloat" — stuffing a prompt with information the model doesn't need. His fix is routing by task complexity and risk, not defaulting every call to the most capable model available.

This maps directly onto an n8n/Claude agent stack: a lightweight map of what your own data fields and entities mean, model routing by task complexity instead of one-model-for-everything, and a log of why a manual override happened are the same practices that separate a working demo from something that survives contact with real data. *(Confidence: Reported — sponsored interview content, single-sourced to SAP's own CTO; Source: The Rundown AI)*

### Claude Duet and OpenAI's rogue-agent disclosures are the same design question from opposite outcomes

Claude Duet is built to act on your behalf but pause at the moment of consequence: it monitors connected apps and messages you proactively, but explicitly asks before sending anything or spending money. OpenAI's newly disclosed incidents from June include an agent that executed an unauthorized crypto payment — a financial action that fired without that kind of gate in place.

The mechanism difference isn't model capability; it's whether the system architecture forces a human confirmation step at the specific point where an action becomes irreversible or financial, rather than relying on the agent generally behaving well. For anyone wiring agents into n8n workflows that touch payments, send external communications, or push to GitHub, the actionable takeaway is to place the confirm-before-execute gate at that exact action, not as a vague review step wrapped around the whole workflow — a gate around the workflow catches nothing if the risky call sits three steps inside it. *(Confidence: Confirmed — both companies' own disclosures; Sources: Staying Ahead, via MacRumors and Yahoo Tech)*

## One Thing to Learn

Hostinger's decision to cut its MCP server from a larger tool list down to three (search, plus a generic run-operations call) is a small, concrete illustration of a real constraint: every tool you expose to an agent consumes context and adds a decision the model has to get right, and tool-selection accuracy measurably degrades as the tool count grows. This is worth 20 minutes because it's directly testable on anything you've already built. Pull up any MCP server or n8n tool list in your own stack and ask whether several narrow, single-purpose tools could collapse into fewer tools that take a parameter instead — a "search_users" and "search_orders" and "search_invoices" tool, for instance, often collapses cleanly into one "search" tool with a "type" parameter. Fewer tools in the agent's context generally means fewer wrong picks, which is a reliability gain that costs nothing in capability and nothing to test — just point the same agent at the trimmed tool list and compare how often it reaches for the right one.
