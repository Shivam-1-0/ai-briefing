## Worth Reading Closely

**Zombie stats are quietly corrupting AI answers — and that's a grounding problem, not a chatbot problem.** A team at Trakkr set out to check a number that's been repeated for years — that 75% of people never click past Google's first page — and couldn't find any study it traces back to. So they widened the test: ask several popular AI assistants a batch of questions that require a factual number, then follow each cited stat back to its claimed source. Fewer than half held up. The clearest case is the "94% of first impressions are about design" line that gets quoted as settled fact: it traces back to a single 2004 study where 15 people rated health websites, and even then "design" was just the most common reason people gave for disliking a site, not a measured 94% figure. Two decades of blog posts and slide decks turned a small, specific finding into a universal rule, and because that rule is all over the training data, AI models repeat it with full confidence and no caveat. *(Reported, via TAAFT/Trakkr)*

Why this matters at the mechanism level: the failure isn't the model "lying" — it's that retrieval and language generation don't carry provenance by default. A model trained on text that contains a confidently-stated number has no built-in signal that the number's chain of custody is broken; it reproduces the surface claim because that's the pattern it learned, not because it checked the number against a primary source. For anyone building agentic workflows — including an n8n pipeline that pulls numbers into a report, a sales deck, or a briefing like this one — the practical fix is the same one the Trakkr team used: treat any AI-stated number as a claim, not a fact, until you've asked the model (or a grounding/citation step in your pipeline) to produce the original study, the sample size, and the date. If that chain breaks anywhere, downgrade the claim to "reported" or drop it. This is also a good argument for building a citation-check node into any automation that generates content containing statistics, rather than trusting the model's first answer.

**A research team used an AI coding agent to resurrect two 1960s–80s AI programs — on purpose, as a mirror.** Herbert Simon's 1961 program tried to write code the way a person would: compare what exists to what's needed, pick the step that closes the gap, repeat. A 1984 MIT tool took stilted natural-language commands and assembled them into working code from reusable chunks — a rough ancestor of today's coding assistants. Both had sat unrun for decades. A small team, calling the method "executable archaeology," got both running again using Claude Code to do much of the reconstruction work from old papers and notes — deliberately using a present-day coding AI to revive programs that were themselves early attempts to model how programmers think. Both programs reproduced their original published output: Simon's matched the code from his 1963 paper, and the MIT tool re-ran its own 1984 self-test example. *(Confirmed, via TAAFT/Jeff Shrager et al.)*

Why it matters beyond the novelty: it's a concrete data point on how far an agentic coding tool can get with "reconstruct this system from fragmentary historical documentation" — a task structurally similar to what any team doing legacy-code migration or undocumented-system recovery actually needs, and a useful stress test for how an agent handles ambiguity when there's no one left to ask.

That citation-anchoring pattern (answer plus exact source location, not just a confident paragraph) is the same discipline the zombie-stats story argues for, and it's a pattern worth copying into any n8n agent that summarizes or answers questions over a document set.

## One Thing to Learn

Spend 20 minutes on **claim grounding** — the practice of making an AI system show its source for every factual assertion it produces, not just its conclusion. Today's zombie-stats story is the clearest possible case for why this matters: AI models reproduce confidently-stated numbers from their training data without any built-in check on whether those numbers still trace back to real evidence, so a model can be fluent, well-formatted, and simply wrong at the same time.

For anyone building agentic workflows in n8n, this translates into a concrete pattern: add a step after any LLM node that generates factual claims (stats, quotes, dates, named sources) which forces the model to restate its source for each claim — study name, sample size, date, or a direct quote from retrieved context — in a structured format you can check. If the model can't produce a specific source, treat the claim as unverified and either drop it or flag it before it reaches a report, email, or dashboard. This is a five-minute addition to a workflow that measurably reduces the single most common failure mode of AI-generated content: a confident number nobody checked.
