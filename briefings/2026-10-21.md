## Worth Reading Closely

**AI agents just made mass hacking cheap — and that's the real story.** NorForge found 20+ unsecured control panels for Cairn, an open-source framework for running autonomous AI agents, left exposed online. A Chinese-speaking operator had used them to point agent swarms at a pre-screened list of busy e-commerce sites, feeding each one a target description under 100 characters and letting the agents handle reconnaissance, exploitation, and extraction largely unsupervised. Reuters identified Deere, Cummins, and SharkNinja among the 100+ companies breached out of roughly 1,200 targeted. The mechanism that matters here isn't that AI can hack — it's the economics: NorForge estimates each successful breach cost about $50 in model inference, with a typical intrusion taking under a day using Claude Code and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. A month ago, the notable story was three researchers with Claude breaching OpenAI's own systems as a demonstration. Now it's one person with roughly $5,000 in API spend compromising over a hundred companies. The defensive side hasn't caught up: none of the victim companies confirmed data loss, but none could say for certain either, and the barrier to running an attack like this has dropped to "anyone who can write a one-line prompt." *Confirmed — Rundown (via NorForge research, Reuters).*

**Jack Clark's singularity remarks are a policy signal, not a thought experiment.** Speaking to economists and policymakers at the IMF and World Bank's annual meetings, Clark argued that the point where AI accelerates discovery faster than humans can absorb it has "now begun." The specifics matter more than the label: he cited Humanity's Last Exam (expert-level test questions) moving from an 8% pass rate to 67.7% since 2025, and FrontierMath going from 2% to 94% — a shift in what these systems can verifiably do, not just a vibe. He also referenced an internal Anthropic model projecting $44 trillion in US GDP by 2030 under fast AI diffusion, which he called "outlandish" on its face while still warning of serious near-term job disruption. The part worth tracking as a finance-adjacent reader: Clark said CEO Dario Amodei has floated the idea of an explicit ceiling on how capable models are allowed to become, and that there may only be "a small number of years" to decide on it before the decision is made for everyone by deployment momentum. Whether or not the $44T figure holds up, a frontier lab publicly discussing self-imposed capability limits to policymakers — rather than to a tech conference — is a shift in who this conversation is being aimed at. *Confirmed (statements made) / Reported (economic projections) — Rundown.*

## Agents & Automation Watch

Four items worth tracking if you're building agent workflows:

- **Meta's "thin orchestrator" pattern.** Meta published how it made its internal AI agents more reliable by stripping the router down to pure dispatch — it decides which specialized agent handles a task but never does domain work itself. This is a direct, importable pattern for n8n: keep your routing/decision node dumb and push all actual logic into dedicated sub-workflows, rather than letting one "smart" node accumulate both jobs.
- **Clairic: a non-generative "extraction model."** Cloudflare introduced a new model class that extracts, computes, or forecasts values from unstructured input without generating tokens — so it structurally can't hallucinate a wrong-shaped answer, though it can still misjudge. Confidence scores ship with every output. Worth watching as an alternative to LLM-based structured extraction nodes where hallucination risk currently forces you to add verification steps.
- **"Office Intelligence" for long-running AI work.** A new research project focused on how agents build evidence, produce durable work artifacts, and carry knowledge across tasks rather than starting fresh each run — relevant to anyone whose n8n agents need memory across multi-step jobs rather than per-invocation context.
- **Scientific Agent Skills (open source, via TAAFT).** A free GitHub skill pack that teaches Claude Code, Cursor, or Codex biology- and chemistry-lab-assistant skills — a concrete example of the "skills" pattern for extending coding agents into domain-specific tasks.

## One Thing to Learn

**The thin orchestrator pattern**, from Meta's engineering write-up this week, is worth 20 minutes because it names a failure mode you've probably already hit in n8n: a router node that starts "just checking one more condition" until it's secretly running business logic, and now every change to that logic risks breaking routing for everything else. Meta's fix is a hard rule — the orchestrator's only job is reading a request and dispatching it to the one specialized agent built for it; it holds no domain knowledge and makes no judgment calls about correctness. Each specialized agent is independently testable and replaceable without touching the router. For an n8n-based stack, this maps directly onto structure: one lightweight "switch" workflow that only routes, calling out to separate, single-purpose sub-workflows for each task type. The payoff isn't elegance — it's that you can debug or swap one sub-workflow without re-testing the whole pipeline, which is the actual bottleneck once an agent stack grows past a handful of nodes.

## Startups & Funding Worth Knowing

**Anthropic and Nscale are reportedly negotiating another $45B compute deal** — on top of the $45B compute deal the two companies announced barely a month ago. Both companies are mid-IPO process, which makes the timing notable: stacking two deals of identical size in quick succession reads either as genuine compute demand outstripping what one deal covers, or as a way to keep headline numbers growing heading into public filings. Nscale is reportedly facing investor pushback on its proposed $30B valuation, which makes a second marquee Anthropic deal useful leverage at exactly the moment it needs to defend that number to IPO investors. Anthropic, for its part, is still expected to go public this year and just pledged an initial 5% of its equity (roughly $48B at last valuation) to a new Public Benefit Fund for workers displaced by AI — a commitment that, combined with aggressive compute spending, signals a company trying to look both expansionary and responsible simultaneously ahead of listing. (Elon Musk responded to the Public Benefit Fund news by pledging to give every American child a SpaceX share via Trump Accounts starting next year — a competing-philanthropy signal worth noting if you're tracking how AI-wealth redistribution pledges are becoming a competitive dynamic among lab founders, not just a one-off PR move.)

**Salesforce agreed to buy Braze for roughly $5.7B including debt**, adding a customer-engagement platform built for AI-driven, personalized outreach. The acquisition logic is straightforward: Salesforce has been assembling the pieces to offer "AI agent talks directly to your customers" as a packaged product rather than something customers have to stitch together themselves, and Braze's existing marketing-automation customer base is a faster path to that than building the messaging layer in-house. Watch for how Salesforce prices this bundled into its Agentforce push over the next two quarters — that's the actual test of whether the deal pays for itself.

**Waymo raised $5B from private credit lenders** and plans to grow its fleet from roughly 4,000 vehicles (currently across 15 US cities) to 20,000 by the end of 2027 — a 5x expansion funded largely through debt rather than equity, with thousands of vehicles already ordered from Chinese manufacturers. Financing fleet growth via private credit rather than another equity round is itself a signal: it suggests Waymo (or parent Alphabet) wants to avoid further valuation-setting rounds right now, possibly because unit economics per ride are becoming provable enough to support debt service, or possibly because equity investors are pricier than lenders are willing to be for an asset-backed bet (the cars themselves serve as collateral-like value). Either way, robotaxi fleet math is shifting from "venture-funded experiment" to "financed capital asset," which is the point at which a business model usually has to start actually working.

**AI lab HUMXN is subsidizing plumbing, electrical, HVAC, and carpet-cleaning jobs across Minneapolis, Chicago, and Miami** in exchange for camera footage of the work, to train robots on real home-repair tasks. This is a cheap, clever data-acquisition strategy worth noting as a pattern: rather than paying for data collection directly, HUMXN is paying for a service people already want and collecting the training data as a byproduct, which lowers both its cost-per-data-point and its recruiting friction versus a straight "wear a camera for us" ask. Expect more physical-AI startups to copy this model — subsidized real-world services as a data funnel — rather than running dedicated data-collection operations.
