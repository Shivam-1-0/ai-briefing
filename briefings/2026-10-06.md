## Worth Reading Closely

### OpenAI's safety story is turning into a liability story, not just a PR one

This week stacked three separate signals on top of each other. David Robinson, who spent 3.5 years at OpenAI drafting its Preparedness Framework and overseeing safety reviews for twelve frontier launches, resigned and used his exit essay to say the team was "so busy sprinting" it rarely got to consider — let alone make — structural changes. That follows OpenAI firing three researchers this fall for allegedly passing sensitive information to an outside safety group, and echoes almost word-for-word what Jan Leike said leaving the company's superalignment team in 2024: safety had "taken a backseat to shiny products."

Then Sam Altman, in Politico's debut "Decoded" interview, said the world should accept "some bad things happening" for AI's benefits, naming scams and hacking as examples. Bill Gates had just told Meet the Press that AI could help bad actors design something worse than smallpox, and pushed for Congress to legally mandate monitoring rather than trust labs to self-police. Congress didn't act either way — both chambers recessed for the midterms, after Senators Warren and King failed to get colleagues to stay and pass anything.

Here's the mechanism that actually matters: with federal legislation off the table until at least the new year, the pressure that would normally go through Congress is rerouting through two channels that don't need it. The first is local government — the same day as Altman's interview, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta testified under oath before the New York City Council (three only after subpoena threats; SpaceX's AI unit skipped entirely). Cities and states can move on AI the moment federal lawmakers stall, and several already are. The second channel is civil liability. Altman's own framing — that scams and hacking are an acceptable cost of AI's benefits — hands plaintiffs' lawyers a clean argument: if a company's CEO says a harm is an accepted cost, who accepted it on behalf of the person who got scammed? That's a question for a courtroom, not a statute, and it doesn't wait for a legislative calendar.

For anyone building on these labs' APIs, the practical read isn't "regulation is coming" — it's "enforcement is shifting to courts, state AGs, and city councils before it's in a federal rulebook," which means compliance obligations could show up faster and more unevenly (state by state, city by city) than a single federal law would have produced. **Confirmed** core facts (resignation, interview quotes, recess); the liability-shift prediction is **Speculative**. — Rundown AI, Uncovering AI / The Atlantic, Politico, The Guardian, CNBC.

### OpenAI's Decisions API: a cheaper gate in front of your agent's expensive model calls

Most production AI systems don't actually need an LLM for most of the calls they make. Routing a support ticket to "billing," "technical," "account," or "sales," deciding whether an agent should call a tool or answer directly, picking which model handles a request — these all have a small, known set of possible answers. The standard approach today still sends the full input to a general model, asks for JSON back, and parses out one word. OpenAI's Decisions API, previewed at DevDay and now in limited access, is built specifically for this: you hand it the input, a question, and the list of allowed answers, and it returns one of them — without routing through a full generative call.

The mechanism that makes this matter is cost and control, not novelty. OpenAI's current three-tier lineup spans roughly 100x in price — its cheapest model (Luna) runs about $0.10 per million input tokens versus about $10 for its top-tier model (Astra), with the same 100x gap on output. Most apps default to sending everything to the strongest model they can afford because routing logic is extra work. A decision layer flips that: it only has to answer "is this request simple, complex, or high-stakes?" and your app sends the request to the model tier that actually matches. Inside an agent loop, the same logic applies to the "what next" step — instead of asking a full reasoning model to replan after every action, a decision layer picks from the agent's small set of known next moves (answer, search, call a tool, escalate), and the reasoning model only gets invoked for the parts of the task that genuinely need it.

The caveat is real: OpenAI hasn't published the endpoint, request format, pricing, or latency yet, so any code claiming to call `openai.decisions.create()` today is not working code. What you can build now, and what's worth doing for an n8n-based agent stack, is the boundary the real API will slot into — a single function (say, `decideRoute()`) that today runs on Structured Outputs against a cheap model and tomorrow swaps in Decisions without touching the rest of the workflow. Until pricing lands, don't assume Decisions beats a well-scoped Structured Outputs call on a small model; benchmark both once access opens. **Reported** on the API's existence and preview status; its cost/latency advantage over Structured Outputs is **Speculative** until OpenAI publishes numbers. — Vaibhav Sisinty.

## One Thing to Learn

Andrej Karpathy's "airplane manual trick," worth the full 20 minutes: when an AI's answer comes back as five dense paragraphs, paste it back in and ask the model to rewrite it in ASD-STE100 — the controlled-English standard written in the 1980s for aircraft maintenance manuals, so mechanics couldn't misread instructions under pressure. Short sentences, one idea per sentence, no ambiguous pronouns. Every major model already knows the standard; you're just invoking it. If the result reads too clipped, Karpathy's fix is to ask the model to go "80% of the way" instead of fully compliant. He also layers on asking for a diagram when text alone isn't landing, or "give me this as HTML" when you want something clickable rather than scrollable. The underlying skill isn't prompting — it's noticing that getting a response is only half the job, and that asking for a different format costs one extra sentence against a real chunk of reading time saved. Try it on the next long explanation you get back, especially anything with nested conditionals or multi-step logic, which is exactly where restructuring into short, standard-English steps exposes gaps the original paragraph form was hiding. **Confirmed** — TAAFT / Andrej Karpathy.

## Rotating: Startups & Funding Worth Knowing

Today's funding and company news is thinner than the safety and policy news, but four moves are worth tracking because they show where capital and infrastructure bets are actually landing right now.

**Prime Intellect's "Prime Inference" platform.** Prime Intellect — a startup building open frontier models and what it calls an "open superintelligence stack" — launched serverless and reserved-capacity serving for open-weight models across its own GPU infrastructure. The company says the same platform already handles close to a trillion tokens a day internally, covering large-scale reinforcement-learning rollouts, synthetic data generation, evals, and long-running coding agents. The significance isn't the product announcement itself; it's the business model underneath it. Serving infrastructure for open models is becoming its own category separate from training them, which means a startup can compete on inference economics and reliability without having to out-train OpenAI or Anthropic on frontier capability. For anyone evaluating open-weight models for cost reasons, this is a sign the "who hosts it reliably" problem is getting solved by specialists rather than staying a DIY exercise. **Confirmed** — TLDR AI.

**Aleph Alpha's Kolibri release.** The German AI company released Kolibri, an open-weight English-German mixture-of-experts model with 78.1 billion parameters, a one-million-token context window, tool-calling support, and an Apache 2.0 license on Hugging Face. Aleph Alpha is explicitly positioning this for "sovereign, mission-critical work in government and regulated industries" — i.e., customers who need to run a capable model on infrastructure they fully control, for reasons that are regulatory and geopolitical as much as technical. This is the same logic as the "smaller models are the future of AI sovereignty" argument TLDR AI also ran today: countries and regulated industries are increasingly treating model choice as a sovereignty question, not just a capability-per-dollar question, and European vendors like Aleph Alpha are building their whole go-to-market around being the answer to that question. **Confirmed** — TLDR AI.

**Anthropic's $100 million bet on talent, not just models.** Anthropic is putting $100 million into the Claude Frontier Academy, a program aiming to train 10,000 AI engineers by 2027, with enterprise partners including Accenture and Morgan Stanley. This isn't R&D spending or a funding round — it's Anthropic buying enterprise distribution the slow way, by building the pipeline of people who will specify, deploy, and troubleshoot Claude inside large companies. Partnering with a systems integrator (Accenture) and a bank (Morgan Stanley) specifically signals where Anthropic expects its revenue to come from: large regulated enterprises that need in-house expertise before they'll commit budget, not self-serve API usage. Worth watching whether OpenAI or Google answer with a similarly sized enterprise-training commitment, since this kind of program is cheap to copy and expensive to ignore if a competitor locks up the same consulting and banking relationships first. **Confirmed** — TLDR AI.

**The valuation backdrop behind the SpaceXSI rename.** Easy to miss inside today's renaming story: SpaceX's AI division has only existed since July, when SpaceX acquired xAI in a deal valuing the combined entity at $1.25 trillion — and this is already its second identity change in under a year. Whatever one thinks of the "Super Intelligence" rebrand itself, the underlying number is the real signal: capital is willing to value a seven-month-old corporate entity at $1.25 trillion purely on the strength of frontier-model ambition and launch infrastructure combined. That's the scale mega-cap AI infrastructure plays are now operating at, and it's a useful reference point for how far apart "frontier lab infrastructure" and "tools an indie builder assembles in n8n" really are — different games, different capital requirements, and largely different risk profiles, even though both get called "AI startups." **Confirmed** — Uncovering AI / Fox Business.

**Net for an agent-stack builder:** none of today's four items are directly actionable the way the Decisions API write-up above is, but together they say open-model serving infrastructure, sovereignty-positioned open-weight releases, and enterprise-training partnerships are where new capital is concentrating this week — worth knowing if you're choosing which ecosystem to build dependencies on, even if nothing here changes what you ship this week.
