## Worth Reading Closely

### Anthropic's Fable 5.2 swarm shows what "pacing, not pausing" actually bought it

Anthropic framed Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 as the company deliberately slowing its own pace — "we called for pacing, not pausing," per exec Dianne Penn — yet the result still took the top spot on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index, 5 points ahead of a Gemini 4 Carbon that Google hasn't even released yet. Mechanically, what changed: 5.2 is 20% cheaper and up to 30% faster to generate than 5.1, uses fewer tokens to finish hard tasks (more work per API budget), and wrongly refuses 55% fewer requests than its predecessor. It still isn't cheap — $8 in / $40 out per million tokens, twice Opus 5.5's price — but it now does more per token than before.

The demonstration mattered more than the benchmark. Anthropic split a single task — designing an operating system from an empty folder — across 1,000 copies of Fable running in parallel for 8 days straight, each instance writing and reviewing the others' code. The result booted on real hardware with its own kernel, storage, networking drivers, login screen, and installer. A separate swarm wrote a full formal proof of a well-known math theorem in 30 hours; the same task previously took 11 days with a human guiding the process. Mythos — the same model minus the guardrail loosening, restricted to vetted security and life-sciences partners — predicted the shape of gene-therapy delivery proteins correctly in 28 of 34 attempts.

What this means for anyone building on n8n or Claude's API: the headline isn't "a smarter model," it's "a model cheap and fast enough that running 1,000 parallel copies of it on one project is now a thing a company actually does, not a thought experiment." Anthropic hasn't published how the agents' work was checked beyond having other agents review it — worth remembering before trusting any output from a long unsupervised multi-agent run, your own included. *(Confidence: Confirmed on pricing/benchmarks — Anthropic, Artificial Analysis; corroborated by The Rundown AI, TLDR AI, TAAFT.)*

### The tofu test and the model-substitution problem

Vaibhav Sisinty's newsletter this week is about a specific, boring-sounding risk that matters to anyone paying for API access through anything other than the official Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini apps: the company sitting between you and the model can silently swap in a cheaper one and keep charging you full price. This already happened in the open-model world — when Moonshot tested third-party hosts serving its Kimi model, some botched 28% of tool calls that the original got right. Closed models like Claude can't be altered by a reseller, but a router sitting in front of the API can quietly send your request to a cheaper model instead, and you'd have no way to tell from the response alone.

Anthropic's answer is FlashProve, built with cryptographers from EPFL, MIT, and StarkWare and released on GitHub: it attaches a cryptographic receipt to an answer proving which specific model produced it, without exposing the model's internals. The catch is real — it's explicitly a research prototype marked "not maintained," only tested on Meta's open Llama 4 Maverick, handles chats of roughly 750 words, costs 70–95x the compute of the original answer, and only proves which model answered, not whether the answer is true.

Until receipts are standard, Vaibhav's practical fix is a "tofu test": three deliberately hard prompts (a shared spreadsheet has the prompts and answer key) run twice each on the official app and on whatever tool you actually pay for. If the official app passes and your tool doesn't, you have a specific, actionable complaint for support — ask which model and version answered, not whether it feels dumber, since the chatbot itself usually doesn't know its own version. For anyone routing Claude calls through third-party infrastructure in an n8n stack, this is worth running once now and again monthly, since model swaps rarely come with an announcement. *(Confidence: Confirmed mechanism and test method — Vaibhav Sisinty, citing Anthropic's FlashProve research and Moonshot's vendor-verifier data.)*

## One Thing to Learn

**Test-time scaling, and why there are two different kinds.** "Test-time scaling" gets used as one phrase for two different things, and the difference changes how you should think about any "thinking" or "extended reasoning" toggle you flip in an API call. The first kind lets a model trade more compute at inference for more performance on a task, but only up to a ceiling set during training — you can make it think longer, but it caps out at whatever reasoning depth it practiced. The second kind has no such ceiling: it lets a model reach performance levels that never showed up during training at all, because the scaling comes from search strategies layered on top of the base model, not just "more of the same thinking."

It's worth 20 minutes because it names something you've probably already noticed but couldn't explain: why cranking up a "thinking budget" sometimes barely moves a result, and other times unlocks a task the model couldn't do at all. If you're choosing between a fast/cheap mode and an extended-reasoning mode for a step in an n8n pipeline, knowing which kind of scaling that toggle actually buys you is the difference between spending tokens and wasting them. *(Source: TLDR AI, citing Forethought's research essay)*

## Rotating: Startups & Funding Worth Knowing

### Anthropic is pushing its IPO forward into a shaky market

The Wall Street Journal reports Anthropic intends to proceed with its IPO despite current jitters across AI stocks, with shares potentially trading as soon as next Thursday. The timing is notable: Nvidia just lost roughly $190B in market value over CEO Jensen Huang's spy-adjacent scandal, and investors are visibly re-pricing AI-adjacent risk across the board. Anthropic choosing to launch into that environment rather than wait suggests the company either has investor demand already locked in, or doesn't expect the broader jitters to ease soon. For anyone tracking the AI funding cycle as a leading indicator, a successful Anthropic IPO into a nervous market would be a stronger signal than one timed for calm conditions — and a weak debut would say the opposite. *(Reported — The Rundown AI, via WSJ)*

### The gap between what Anthropic spends on compute and what it earns

TLDR AI's deep-dive analysis estimates Anthropic needs roughly $200 billion in annual revenue to support its compute commitments through 2030 at its current spending ratios — against a total compute commitment already at $895 billion. That gap is the whole risk in one sentence: Anthropic has locked in infrastructure spending at a scale that assumes revenue grows a great deal from here, and the analysis flags specific failure modes if it doesn't — demand shortfalls, margin compression from discounting to keep usage up, delivery delays on data-center buildout, and getting stuck buying expensive short-term capacity whenever demand outpaces what was already contracted. None of this is a prediction that Anthropic fails — it's a specific, falsifiable set of numbers to watch. If Anthropic's reported revenue run-rate isn't tracking toward that $200B figure well before 2030, the compute commitment becomes a liability rather than a bet that paid off. For students watching this market, it's a clean example of how a spending commitment that sounds enormous and a revenue number that sounds enormous aren't automatically the same thing. *(Reported, TLDR AI's own analysis of public filings)*

### Fireworks' breach lands days before a $30B+ fundraise

Open-source AI hosting startup Fireworks confirmed a hack that exposed billing details and parts of some customers' models and datasets, days before the company planned to start raising at a valuation above $30 billion. Separately, TLDR AI reported that the underlying October security incident has been traced to a financially motivated cybercriminal group that sells unauthorized LLM access through illicit routing gateways — the same "unauthorized model access for resale" problem underlying the model-substitution risk covered above, just on the criminal end instead of the "quietly cheaper model" end. For a startup, a security incident surfacing in the same news cycle as a funding round is close to the worst possible timing: due-diligence conversations now have a concrete, recent incident to ask about instead of a hypothetical one. Whether the raise proceeds at the same valuation is the number worth watching next. *(Confirmed breach, reported fundraise plans — The Rundown AI, TLDR AI)*

If Meta can combine "cheapest viable model at every tier" with "already in the room via the consultants enterprises already trust," that's a different competitive threat to Anthropic and OpenAI than "has a cheap model" alone. *(Reported — The Rundown AI)*

### Adobe locks in a custom-model deal with Disney instead of selling off-the-shelf access

Adobe signed a multi-year deal with Disney to build custom AI models trained specifically on Disney's characters and visual styles, with work already underway across theme parks, film, and ESPN. The structure is the detail worth noting: this isn't Disney buying seats on a general-purpose tool, it's Adobe building and presumably retaining ongoing commercial rights to IP-specific models for one of the most protective rights-holders in media — the kind of bespoke, defensible deal that's harder for a cheaper competitor to simply undercut on price. For founders thinking about AI business models beyond "sell API access," this is a working example of the alternative: trade a lower unit margin for a locked-in, differentiated, multi-year contract with a client who has every incentive to keep the model's training data proprietary. *(Confirmed — The Rundown AI)*
