## Worth Reading Closely

### Claude's run at a scorpion antivenom shows where the AI-science bottleneck actually sits

Five staffers from investment research firm Citrini Research, most with no lab background, wanted to test an internal, unreleased version of Claude on real drug discovery. Anthropic's own safety filters blocked the biology work when they tried it remotely, so the team had to relocate into Anthropic's offices to run it under staff supervision. From five human messages, Claude then worked unsupervised for 64 hours, spinning up more than 300 parallel copies of itself. It chose which scorpion venom toxins to target, picked the exact binding sites on each, and generated 318,484 candidate binder molecules — proteins shaped to lock onto a toxin like a key cut for one lock. It narrowed that pool to 211 designs and sent them to Twist Bioscience and Adaptyv Bio for physical synthesis and lab testing.

The mechanism-level result: 17 of 211 designs bound their target toxin in the lab, with the strongest holding on comparably to an antibody already known to work. But a true antivenom needs one molecule effective against several toxins at once, and of 91 attempts at that cross-reactivity, only one qualified — against a single toxin, not several. Nothing has been tested for whether any design actually neutralizes venom inside a cell, let alone in an animal, and Anthropic is explicit that it has not built an antivenom. What changed isn't the end result — it's the loop. Design and candidate generation, historically a multi-year specialist task, compressed into a long weekend of compute directed by non-experts. The slow part moved entirely to physical lab validation, which still runs on biology's timeline, not a model's. *(Confidence: Confirmed, published by Anthropic; corroborated independently by The Rundown AI and TAAFT.)*

### SpaceX's AI unit reveals how fragile the compute-rental trade really is

SpaceX's "Super Intelligence" AI division grew revenue more than 9x and now accounts for over half of group sales. Q3 revenue hit $14.6B against a $12.7B estimate, with $2.8B in operating profit versus a $273M loss a year earlier. The business model is straightforward: build data centers, then rent GPU compute on short-term contracts that reprice upward every few months while supply stays scarce. But free cash outflow hit $10B in the quarter against $19.6B of capex, and Q4 capex guidance runs $35-40B — Hargreaves Lansdown estimates SpaceX will need to raise fresh capital within six months just to fund the buildout it has already promised.

The mechanism that matters: short-term contracts are the reason margins look spectacular right now, but they cut both ways. The moment supply catches up with demand, those same contracts reprice downward just as fast, with no long-term commitment to cushion the fall — exactly what hit Korean data-center firm Elice on its IPO debut this week. On the other side of SpaceX's compute bill sits its customers: Anthropic's IPO prospectus disclosed an $80.7B net loss for H1 2026, but roughly $72B of that is a non-cash markup from revaluing convertible notes as the company's valuation rises. The number that actually matters is buried underneath — Anthropic's compute spend hit about $9B in six months, up from $2.3B a year earlier, against $16.3B in revenue. Every dollar a lab like Anthropic burns on compute is revenue for an infrastructure landlord like SpaceX. The entire trade holds together only as long as labs keep growing revenue fast enough to justify ever-larger compute budgets — the day one major lab slows its spending, every short-term contract in the market reprices at once. *(Confidence: Confirmed, from Q3 earnings and IPO filing data; source: Uncovering AI, citing Hargreaves Lansdown analysis and WSJ reporting on Anthropic's S-1.)*

## One Thing to Learn

The Rundown AI published a concrete tutorial on using Claude Code hooks to make the tool check its own work after every edit, rather than trusting it to self-report. The walkthrough has you build a small test suite, add a hook that reformats a settings file right after Claude writes it, then layer in the same test suite so it runs automatically after each edit. To prove the hook actually catches problems, you deliberately introduce a bug and watch the hook's test fail before you fix anything — then confirm the fix passes without touching the test itself.

This is worth 20 minutes specifically because it's infrastructure, not a one-off trick: a hook that runs checks after every edit is the difference between an agent that reports success and an agent whose work is actually verified, which matters directly for anyone wiring Claude Code into unattended n8n pipelines. Once the pattern clicks — hook fires on an event, runs a fixed check, fails loud — it extends to linting, schema validation, or any other guardrail a coding agent shouldn't be trusted to self-police.

## Rotating: Research With Practical Implications for Agents, Coding, and Reliability

**Memory is the cheapest reliability win available right now.** Databricks' MemCurrent is a persistent memory architecture for production agents, and the claim worth taking seriously is specific: it triples an agent's pass rate over a no-memory baseline while making the agent both faster and cheaper to run, not slower or pricier as "more context" usually implies. The practical implication for anyone building on n8n is that memory architecture — what gets stored, retrieved, and discarded between agent calls — is a lever independent of model choice. A weaker model with well-designed persistent memory can plausibly outperform a stronger model that starts cold on every run. For a workflow that calls Claude repeatedly across a multi-step automation, the question worth asking isn't "which model" but "what does this agent remember between steps, and how is that retrieval scoped."

**Durability is the reliability failure mode nobody designs for until it bites.** Vercel's durable-harness tackles a specific, common failure: an agent session dies mid-turn — the browser tab closes, the process crashes, the connection drops — and the whole task has to restart from zero, burning tokens and losing partial progress. Its fix is to log agent state continuously and resume execution from that log on a fresh process, rather than treating a dropped connection as a dropped task. It works with Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, and Pi, either hosted on Vercel or self-hosted against Postgres. For an n8n-based agent stack, the direct lesson is architectural: any long-running agent call should assume the underlying connection can die mid-execution, and the workflow should be designed to resume from a checkpoint rather than re-run the entire chain — the same pattern n8n's own workflow retries should be built around, not just the LLM call itself.

**Ramp's write-up on building Laminar is the closest thing to a reference blueprint for a serious agent harness.** The notable detail isn't that the code was agent-generated in six weeks — it's what the harness actually needed once it existed: secure storage of conversation history, session compaction (so long-running agent conversations don't blow past context limits), standardized interfaces for tool calling, message queuing and steering (so a human or another process can redirect an agent mid-task), support for subagents, multiplayer chat (several humans or agents in the same session), and observability tooling to see what the agent actually did. Ramp's own conclusion is worth sitting with: building in-house has gotten cheaper, but it still needs experienced engineers on call, and the hard part isn't writing the harness — it's knowing what product teams actually need from it. For someone building smaller n8n-based agent workflows, this list is a checklist of what tends to go missing first: session compaction and observability are the two most commonly skipped until something breaks in production and nobody can tell why.

**METR's review of Anthropic's own misalignment report is a useful check on how much to trust an AI lab's self-assessment of its agents' reliability.** METR largely agrees that today's models carry low misalignment risk — but specifically because their capabilities are still limited, not because safeguards are robust. The sharper finding is that current evaluation benchmarks don't reliably test whether a model can deceive the very monitoring systems meant to catch it, and that real-world incidents of undetected misalignment have already surfaced, which cuts against confident claims of "low risk." METR's recommendation is more comprehensive red-teaming and wider risk frameworks going forward. The practical takeaway for anyone deploying agents, even at a much smaller scale than a frontier lab: an agent passing your eval suite today tells you about today's capabilities, not about what happens once the agent gets better at tasks your eval suite wasn't designed to catch — which argues for red-teaming your own automations periodically rather than treating a passing test suite as a permanent guarantee.

**Separating what a model knows from how it reasons is the idea behind Metis Reasoning's "Age of Reasoning" architecture, and it maps directly onto how to debug an agent chain.** Built by ex-DeepMind researcher Thore Graepel and inspired by AlphaGo's split between learned policy and verified search, the architecture explicitly tracks three things separately as a model reasons: which claims are backed by verified evidence, how uncertain each step is, and what reasoning strategy produced the conclusion. The stated goals are fewer hallucinations, conclusions that can actually be audited after the fact, and better generalization to domains the model wasn't trained on. The practical implication for someone wiring Claude into an n8n pipeline is a design pattern, not a product to adopt wholesale: have the agent output its evidence sources and confidence level as separate, inspectable fields rather than folding them into a single prose answer. That turns "the agent said X" into "the agent said X, based on Y, with Z confidence" — which is the difference between a black-box step in an automation and one you can actually debug when it's wrong. *(Confirmed, covered independently by TLDR AI and The Rundown AI.)*
